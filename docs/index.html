Project Overview

Mountain meadows in the Cascade-Siskiyou Bioregion provide multiple critical ecosystem services that are becoming increasingly vital due to climate change. These ecosystems function as natural sponges, retaining water during wet seasons and slowly releasing it through summer dry periods, sustaining late-season stream flows when they're needed most. They sequester significant amounts of carbon, helping mitigate greenhouse gas emissions. They also support exceptional biodiversity, providing habitat for diverse plant and animal communities.

Historically, the Cascade-Siskiyou region's Mediterranean climate relied on snowpack accumulation and groundwater to maintain meadow ecosystems and sustain late-summer flows. However, as temperatures rise and precipitation patterns shift from snow-dominated to rain-dominated systems, groundwater recharge is declining and meadows are drying out. Human activities have compounded these climate pressures: stream incision has lowered water tables, and the historical loss of beavers has reduced natural wetland saturation. Many meadows have degraded significantly, with upland vegetation encroaching as water tables drop and hydrologic function deteriorates.

With appropriate mapping and modeling tools, natural resource managers could systematically identify degraded "lost" meadows across the landscape and prioritize them for restoration based on their potential to restore hydrologic function. They could evaluate how different climate scenarios, such as reduced snowpack or increased drought, might impact meadow persistence and their connectivity to stream systems. This will allow for data-driven decisions about where restoration investments would yield the greatest benefits for water storage, carbon sequestration, and biodiversity.
